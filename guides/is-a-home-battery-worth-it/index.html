Is a Home Battery Worth It in 2026? The Payback Math, Honestly

Home battery payback in 2026 typically lands between 7 and 15 years — 6–9 in California under NEM 3.0, and often never in flat-rate states. See the scenarios, then run your own numbers.

9 min read Updated November 2026 Sources linked at the bottom

The honest answer: it depends on three things — what your utility charges at different hours, which incentives you can actually claim, and what backup power is worth to you. In 2026, typical payback runs 7–15 years. In some markets the bill-savings math never pays back at all, and “never” is a real answer.

The one-sentence version A battery is a good deal when the gap between your cheap hours and expensive hours is wide, your incentives are real, and you value outage protection. It is a poor deal on a flat, low rate with no rebates — no matter how good the battery is.

Three things that changed in 2026

  • The 30% federal credit ended. For owner-purchased systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, there is no federal credit. Quotes that still assume it overstate the net benefit by about 30% of the gross cost. Full battery tax credit 2026 explainer →
  • California’s NEM 3.0 cut export credits. Excess solar exported to the grid now earns roughly $0.05–$0.08/kWh — about 75% less than under NEM 2.0. A battery lets you use that daytime solar during expensive evening hours instead, which is why solar-plus-battery payback in California now beats solar alone (roughly 6–9 years versus 10–14).
  • Electricity rates keep climbing. The U.S. average residential rate was about 18.3¢/kWh in November 2026, up roughly 5% year over year. Higher rates widen the arbitrage opportunity — and make outages more expensive, too.

The math, with one worked example

Take a 13.5 kWh battery that shifts about 10–12 kWh per day. If your cheap-to-expensive spread is 25¢/kWh, that is roughly $900–$1,100 a year in bill savings before efficiency losses. At $15,000 installed with no incentives, that is a 14–16 year payback — longer than the typical 10-year warranty.

Now change the inputs: a 40¢ spread and a $2,000 local rebate pull payback under 10 years. The same battery, a completely different decision. That is why “are batteries worth it?” has no single answer.

  • Round-trip efficiency of about 90% means you cannot shift every kWh you charge.
  • Degradation reduces capacity over time; most warranties guarantee about 70% at year 10.
  • Cycle limits cap how aggressively you can arbitrage without eating into warranty headroom.

When a battery makes sense even if payback is slow

  • You live where outages or public safety shutoffs are frequent or dangerous.
  • Your utility’s time-of-use spread is wide and likely to widen.
  • You have solar on NEM 3.0 and export credits are minimal — self-consumption is the game; model your export vs import with the NEM 3.0 Arbitrage Calculator.
  • A documented VPP or utility program pays you for grid services, and enrollment is actually open.
  • You value resilience enough to pay for it, the same way you would for insurance.

And when it does not: a flat, low rate; no outage concern; and any plan that depends on a federal credit that no longer exists.

Quick answers

Do home batteries pay for themselves?

Sometimes. In high-rate areas with time-of-use pricing and real incentives, payback can land inside the 10-year warranty. On flat, low rates, bill savings alone often never recover the cost — the battery’s value is backup power instead.

How long do home batteries last?

Most current home batteries carry a 10-year warranty with roughly 70% capacity guaranteed at the end. Some models advertise longer or higher retention. Payback that runs beyond the warranty is a warning sign, not a deal.

Is a battery worth it without solar?

Only where there is a real time-of-use spread to exploit. On a flat rate, a battery without solar has almost no bill savings; it is a backup purchase. California’s blended rate (~34¢) versus peak rates above 55¢ and off-peak under 25¢ is the clearest example of a market where arbitrage works.
