Whole-House Generator Cost in 2026: What You’ll Actually Pay

A whole-house standby generator costs $8,000–$16,000 installed in 2026, plus $450–$1,300 a year in fuel and maintenance. See the size-by-size price ladder, hidden costs, and the 10-year total.

8 min read Updated November 2026 Sources linked at the bottom

A whole-house standby generator is the only backup option that can run your home indefinitely — as long as fuel keeps flowing. That endurance has a price: $8,000–$16,000 installed for a typical unit, plus fuel and maintenance every year whether you use it or not.

Quick answer Budget $8,000–$16,000 installed for a mainstream whole-house unit (Generac’s own range), or roughly $9,000–$12,000 for a 22 kW unit serving a 2,000–3,000 sq ft home. Add $450–$1,300 per year for fuel and service, and plan for a 10-year total of $12,000–$30,000+.

What’s inside the installed price

  • Generator unit — the hardware itself.
  • Automatic transfer switch (ATS) — senses the outage and switches your panel over; often $500–$900+ if priced separately.
  • Fuel connection — tying into a natural gas line or installing a propane tank and line. This can be the largest variable.
  • Electrical work and permits — panel work, conduit, inspection fees.
  • Site prep — concrete pad, clearance from windows and doors, ventilation.

A quote that looks unusually low often excludes the transfer switch, gas line or permits. Ask for everything as line items.

The costs that never stop

Repairs (unbudgeted)VariesBatteries, starters and control boards wear out over a 10–20 year life

Fuel use scales with load and fuel type. A 7 kW unit at full load burns roughly 1.5 gallons of propane per hour or about 140 cubic feet of natural gas per hour. Long outages during a regional event can become the dominant cost — and fuel deliveries can be delayed exactly when you need them.

10-year total cost: the honest range

For a mid-range unit with modest use, 10-year ownership commonly lands at $12,000–$30,000+. The low end assumes few outages, low fuel prices and no major repairs; the high end assumes a whole-house unit, regular outages, and service calls.

Here is the uncomfortable comparison: the generator earns nothing between outages. A home battery can reduce electric bills year-round, and a portable station costs a fraction upfront. That does not make the generator wrong — it makes it a resilience purchase, priced like insurance.

Quote red flags

  • “Installed” price without transfer switch, gas line or permits. Ask for an itemized bid.
  • Sizing by square footage alone. A proper quote checks your actual loads — A/C, well pump, electric heat.
  • No plan for fuel during a regional outage. Natural gas is usually more reliable than delivered propane in storms.
  • Ignoring noise and clearance. Generators are audible and must sit away from windows and doors; some neighborhoods restrict them.
  • Forgetting maintenance. A generator that hasn’t been exercised or serviced is the one that fails when needed.

Is there a cheaper way to get the same coverage?

If your outages are usually hours rather than days, a home battery or a portable station may cover essentials for far less. If you need multi-day whole-home power, the generator remains the only option without a fuel-free recharge source. Our three-way comparison walks through the trade-offs.

Quick answers

How much does a 22 kW Generac cost installed?

Around $9,000–$12,000 for a typical 2,000–3,000 sq ft home in 2026, with higher prices in expensive labor markets or when a long gas run, panel upgrade or propane tank is required.

How long can a standby generator run?

As long as it has fuel. Piped natural gas can run for days; a 100-gallon propane tank typically powers a 10–20 kW unit for roughly 1–3 days at half load. Diesel units usually need refueling every 12–24 hours.

Do generators add home value?

They can be a selling point in outage-prone areas, but there is no reliable, universal resale premium. Treat the purchase as resilience spending, not an investment with a payback.
