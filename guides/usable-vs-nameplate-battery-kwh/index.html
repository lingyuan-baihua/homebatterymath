Usable vs Nameplate kWh: Why Your Battery Delivers Less Than the Label

A 13.5 kWh battery does not deliver 13.5 kWh. Depth of discharge, round-trip efficiency and degradation cut usable capacity — here’s the math and how to size around it.

6 min read Updated November 2026 Sources linked at the bottom

Every home battery is sold by nameplate capacity — the total energy the cells hold. What runs your house is usable capacity, which is smaller. Ignoring the difference is one of the most common sizing mistakes, and it shows up as a battery that runs out sooner than the brochure implied.

Quick rule A 13.5 kWh nameplate battery typically delivers about 10–12 kWh of useful output (after reserve capacity and efficiency). Plan on roughly 75–90% of the label.

What eats the difference

  • Depth of discharge (DoD). Some systems reserve 5–20% to protect longevity. Modern lithium batteries often allow 80–100%, but the spec sheet matters.
  • Round-trip efficiency. About 90% of the energy you put in comes back out; the rest is lost in conversion and heat.
  • Degradation. Capacity fades with cycles and age. Most warranties guarantee about 70% at year 10 — so a decade-old “13.5 kWh” battery may deliver closer to 9.5 kWh.
  • Temperature. Cold garages and extreme heat reduce usable capacity exactly when outages are most likely.

The math on common sizes

Compare those runtimes with the label-based numbers. A 13.5 kWh battery marketed as “13.5 hours at 1 kW” is really about 11 hours at 1 kW once losses are counted — and less as it ages.

How to size around it

  • Ask for usable capacity, not just nameplate kWh, and confirm the DoD the manufacturer allows.
  • Add a buffer if you need guaranteed runtime: round up to the next standard size rather than designing to the exact number.
  • Plan for year-10 capacity, not day-one capacity, if the battery is primarily outage insurance.
  • Check surge ratings for motors — a well pump or A/C can momentarily draw several times its running load.
