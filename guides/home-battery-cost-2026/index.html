Home Battery Cost in 2026: What You’ll Actually Pay

Installed home battery prices in 2026 run $700–$1,300 per kWh — about $10,000–$16,000 for a typical 10–13.5 kWh system. Compare Powerwall 3, Enphase, LG and FranklinWH, and spot quotes built on the expired 30% credit.

8 min read Updated November 2026 Sources linked at the bottom

Installed prices in 2026 cluster around $700–$1,300 per kWh — roughly $10,000–$16,000 for the 10–13.5 kWh systems most homes buy. What you actually pay depends on the brand, how much of the home you back up, and how much electrical work the install requires. And because the federal credit is gone, the number on the quote is the number you pay (minus any state or local rebates).

Quick answer A typical 13.5 kWh battery costs about $13,000–$16,500 installed in the U.S. in 2026 (higher in California). A 10 kWh system runs roughly $9,500–$13,000, and homes adding 20+ kWh of storage should budget $14,000–$26,000.

What moves the price

  • Capacity and unit count. A second unit is usually cheaper per kWh than the first because labor and equipment are shared.
  • Whole-home vs essential-load backup. Backing up the whole panel can require a main panel upgrade, a transfer switch or smart panel — often thousands more.
  • Electrical and site work. Distance from the panel, conduit runs, permitting and inspection costs vary widely by jurisdiction.
  • Labor market and supply. Installer pricing and equipment supply conditions in 2026 have kept installed prices firm even as cell prices fell.
  • Add-ons. Monitoring, critter guards, generator integration, additional circuits and EV charging provisioning.

Quote red flags to watch for

  • A 30% federal credit line item. For a purchased system in 2026, there is no federal credit. If a quote subtracts one, the price you were shown is not the price you will pay. What battery incentives still qualify in 2026 →
  • Hardware $/kWh compared against installed $/kWh. Make sure both quotes include labor, permitting and balance-of-system equipment.
  • “Whole home” promises without a load assessment. Backup capacity depends on what you actually run during an outage.
  • No itemization. Ask for battery, labor, electrical work and adders as separate lines.
  • Discounts that expire today. Urgency is not a pricing strategy you should reward.
  • Ignoring per-market differences. The same battery can vary by thousands of dollars between states — and within a state.
