State Home Battery Incentives in 2026 — What Each State Still Pays

The 30% federal battery credit ended. State, utility and local rebates still pay $300/kWh in Illinois, $500/kWh in California municipal utilities, 25% in New York and more. A state-by-state breakdown with dated sources.

11 min read Updated November 2026 Sources linked at the bottom

With the federal 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) expired for owner-purchased systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, the money that is left for a home battery comes almost entirely from states, utilities and municipalities — and it is distributed very unevenly. A 13.5 kWh battery can earn over $4,000 upfront in Illinois and literally $0 from California's state program. This page breaks down what each major state actually pays in 2026, with the program status and sources so you can verify before you buy.

Bottom line There is no federal tax credit for a battery you buy outright in 2026. State and utility programs range from generous ($300/kWh rebates, 25% state tax credits) to closed or nonexistent. Your utility matters more than your state — two neighbors across a city line can see a $6,000+ difference on the same battery.

For the federal picture — why Section 25D ended and how leases/PPAs can still earn a 30% credit under Section 48E — see our home battery tax credit 2026 guide. This page focuses on the state and utility layer that sits on top.

Quick comparison: eleven states, one 13.5 kWh battery

California — the state program closed, the city programs did not

California is the cautionary tale. SGIP (Self-Generation Incentive Program) was the most discussed battery rebate in the country, and most installer pages still list it as if it were open. As of October 7, 2026, the SGIP budget tracker shows it is not — at least not for standard-income households.

What is closed

  • SGIP Small Residential Storage (general market): paid $0.15/Wh ($150/kWh). Closed at Step 7 in every investor-owned utility territory — PG&E, SCE, SDG&E and SoCalGas. A 13.5 kWh battery that once earned $2,025 now earns $0 here.
  • SGIP Equity Resiliency: paid $1.00/Wh ($1,000/kWh) for income- or medical-need households in fire or PSPS risk areas. Also closed to new reservations.

What is still open

  • SGIP Residential Solar and Storage Equity: pays $1.10/Wh for storage and $3.10/W for solar for low-income homes — but the main PG&E and SCE slices are waitlisted, with only a small slice for publicly-owned-utility customers still open. If you are income-qualified, get on the waitlist; budgets reopen when reservations are cancelled.
  • Property tax exclusion (Section 73): a solar-plus-storage system completed before January 1, 2027 is excluded from property tax reassessment until the home is sold. On a typical system this avoids roughly $5,000+ of tax over 20 years — currently the single most valuable broad incentive in California, and the one most homeowners miss.
  • Municipal utility rebates: city-owned utilities are where the money moved. SMUD pays about $5,400 per Powerwall 3 (nameplate kWh × 80% × $500, capped at $10,000) plus roughly $440/year for peak dispatch. Riverside Public Utilities pays $500/kWh (up to $10,000; $850/kWh for SHARE assistance customers). Pasadena Water and Power pays up to $550/kWh plus $0.60/W for solar. These do not apply if you are on PG&E, SCE or SDG&E.

The takeaway: if a California quote subtracts an SGIP rebate for a standard-income household on an investor-owned utility in 2026, the quote is wrong. Check your specific utility — not a generic "California incentives" list — before counting on anything.

New York — a state tax credit that survived

New York is one of the few states where a meaningful state income tax credit survived the end of the federal credit. The Solar Energy System Equipment Credit is 25% of your net system cost, capped at $5,000, claimed on Form IT-255. It applies to solar-plus-storage and standalone storage that meets the program's requirements, and unused credit can be carried forward to future tax years.

  • State tax credit: 25% of system cost, up to $5,000. On a $20,000 battery that is $5,000; on a $15,000 battery, $3,750. This is a real credit against state tax liability, not a rebate — you need enough state tax to absorb it, or you carry it forward.
  • NYSERDA residential storage incentive: a per-kWh incentive for grid-connected home battery storage, with systems sized up to 25 kWh eligible. The incentive flows through participating installers and is typically reflected as a lower installed price.
  • NY-Sun: while primarily a solar program, it shapes the economics of paired solar-plus-storage and reduces the net cost that the 25% credit then applies to.

New York's combination — a surviving state credit plus an active storage rebate — makes it one of the more favorable states for a battery in 2026, especially for solar owners adding storage.

Massachusetts — SMART 3.0 and pay-for-performance

Massachusetts does not hand over a large upfront check for a battery, but it pays over time through two programs that reward storage for grid service.

  • SMART 3.0: the state's solar incentive pays a per-kWh adder for 20 years on top of net metering credits — roughly $0.03/kWh for standard residential systems and $0.06/kWh for low-income households. Pairing a battery with solar can unlock higher SMART incentive rates within the program's structure.
  • Mass Save battery storage: enrolling a home battery earns incentives for allowing your utility to draw on it during summer peak events. This is a pay-for-performance model — you get paid for grid dispatch rather than an upfront rebate.
  • ConnectedSolutions: a demand-response program that pays battery owners for capacity during peak summer hours, similar to California's VPP programs but available in Massachusetts and several other New England states.

The Massachusetts model favors batteries that actually cycle for the grid. If you want a battery purely for backup and never enroll it in a program, the state-level upside is smaller than in New York or Illinois.

Texas — no state tax credit, but utility rebates and a big exemption

Texas has no state income tax, so there is no state solar or battery tax credit. The value sits in utility rebates and a property tax exemption instead — and because Texas is deregulated, your address and your retailer matter a lot.

  • Property tax exemption: 100% of the appraised value of solar and storage property is exempt from property tax — the system does not increase your home's taxable value. On a $20,000 system this avoids roughly $200–$500/year of property tax depending on your rate.
  • Oncor battery rebate: Oncor (covering much of north and west Texas) has offered battery rebates in the range of several thousand dollars for qualifying systems — the pool varies, and not every installer participates. Check Oncor's current program before relying on a figure.
  • Austin Energy: the municipally-owned Austin utility has run a solar rebate of roughly $2,500 for qualifying residential systems; battery incentives shift year to year.
  • Retail buyback plans: in deregulated Texas, competitive retailers offer solar export buyback rates (some up to roughly $0.085/kWh) that make a battery useful for bill-shifting — but the rate you lock depends on the retailer, not the state.

Texas rewards homeowners who pair a battery with a good buyback plan and capture the property tax exemption. Without a state credit, the economics depend heavily on your utility and retail provider.

Connecticut — a rare flat per-kWh rebate

Connecticut's Energy Storage Solutions (ESS) program is one of the more straightforward battery incentives in the country: an upfront rebate per kWh of installed storage, plus ongoing performance payments.

  • Upfront incentive: roughly $250–$600 per kWh of battery capacity, with the total incentive capped at around $16,000 per system. On a 13.5 kWh battery that is roughly $3,375–$8,100 upfront depending on the tier and system configuration.
  • Ongoing payments: the program also pays for allowing the battery to discharge during grid peak events — a performance component layered on top of the upfront rebate.
  • Eligibility: the battery must be paired with solar (or meet the program's standalone rules) and be enrolled in the program through a participating installer.

ESS is administered by the state's Green Bank together with the utilities, and it is one of the few programs where a standard-income homeowner can still get a meaningful flat per-kWh rebate — the kind California's SGIP used to offer.

Illinois — $300/kWh, with a catch

Illinois has one of the most attractive battery rebates in the country on paper: $300 per kWh of storage, paid as a one-time Distributed Generation rebate by ComEd and Ameren Illinois. On a 13.5 kWh battery that is about $4,050 — more than California's old SGIP general-market rate paid.

  • The rebate: $300/kWh for battery storage and $300/kW for solar with a qualifying smart inverter, paid by ComEd (northern Illinois) or Ameren (central and southern Illinois). A typical battery-only rebate lands near $4,050; a paired solar-plus-storage system earns both.
  • The catch (June 2026 rule): a 2026 state rule added a roughly 5-year retention requirement — you must keep the system in service and enrolled in a Qualifying Utility Product (QUP) demand-response program for about five years, or the rebate is subject to clawback. Treat it as a commitment, not free money.
  • Eligibility: the battery needs an approved smart inverter and you must enroll in your utility's QUP program. The rebate is paid through participating installers and applied to your project.

Illinois is the clearest example of why 2026 is not uniformly bad for batteries: the federal credit disappeared, but several states actually expanded or maintained per-kWh rebates that rival the old federal value.

Maryland — a surviving 30% state storage credit

Maryland is one of the few states where a residential energy storage income tax credit survived the federal credit's end. It is effectively the state-level successor to what Section 25D used to do nationally.

  • Energy Storage Income Tax Credit: 30% of installation cost, capped at $5,000 for residential systems (commercial is capped at $75,000, with a $750,000 annual program total). It is awarded first-come, first-served — once the annual budget is claimed, it is gone until the next round.
  • Residential and Commercial Energy Storage (RCES) grant: a separate state grant covering part of the cost of a new home battery, up to $5,000 for residential projects, administered through the Maryland Energy Administration. Application windows open and close by fiscal year — the FY26 window had already closed as of this writing, so check for the next round.
  • Stacking: the tax credit and the RCES grant are separate programs, but confirm with the administrator whether both can apply to the same battery before counting on the sum.

On a $15,000 battery, a 30% credit capped at $5,000 is a real chunk of payback — Maryland is one of the better states for a standard-income buyer in 2026.

Oregon — per-kWh rebates from two programs

Oregon layers a state program on top of utility-administered Energy Trust of Oregon (ETO) incentives, giving homeowners two separate shots at battery rebates.

  • Energy Trust of Oregon (ETO): pays a per-kWh incentive that depends on your utility — roughly $400/kWh for PGE customers (up to $5,000) and about $320/kWh for Pacific Power customers. A 13.5 kWh battery earns roughly $3,240–$5,400 depending on which utility serves you.
  • Oregon Solar + Storage Rebate Program (ODOE): a state rebate of up to $5,000 for solar plus up to $2,500 for an energy storage system, for homeowners who pair storage with a qualifying solar installation. Amounts and availability change by funding round — the program had a mid-2026 update.
  • Ongoing payments: ETO's Smart Batteries utility reward programs pay additional monthly credits for letting your utility dispatch the battery during peak events.

Oregon is a two-layer state: ETO for the upfront per-kWh rebate, ODOE for the solar+storage pairing rebate. Check both — many homeowners only learn about one of them.

Hawaii — the 35% state credit and the highest electricity rates

Hawaii has the highest residential electricity rates in the United States, which means a battery that shifts solar into the evening earns more per kWh there than anywhere else. The state credit helps with the upfront cost.

  • Renewable Energy Technologies Income Tax Credit (RETITC): a Hawai'i state tax credit of 35% of system cost, capped at $5,000 for a single-family solar PV system. Since 2026, retrofitting an existing solar system with battery storage qualifies for the credit as well — you don't need to buy new panels.
  • Hawaiian Electric Battery Bonus: a cash incentive plus ongoing bill credits for customers who add energy storage to an existing or new rooftop solar system. Reported figures include roughly $850 per kW of solar consumed after the battery is installed — verify the current schedule with Hawaiian Electric, as terms change.
  • Why it matters here: at $0.30–$0.45/kWh retail rates, the same battery that barely pencils in a low-rate state can pay back quickly in Hawai'i — the incentive is the cherry on top, not the whole sundae.

Arizona — bill credits, not upfront checks

Arizona has strong solar irradiance but thin state-level battery incentives. The value here comes from ongoing bill credits and exemptions rather than an upfront rebate.

  • APS Storage Rewards: a five-year pilot where APS customers earn bill credits when the battery shares stored energy during high-demand periods. This is a performance program — you get paid over time for grid service, not an upfront check.
  • SRP battery rebate — closed: SRP's battery storage incentive was capped at 4,500 customers and closed to new applicants in November 2024. Any 2026 quote citing an SRP battery rebate is outdated.
  • Property tax exemption: solar and battery storage systems are exempt from property tax assessment in Arizona — the system does not increase your home's taxable value.
  • No statewide battery rebate or tax credit: unlike Illinois or Maryland, Arizona does not offer a per-kWh rebate or a state storage tax credit in 2026.

Arizona rewards the patient — APS's Storage Rewards pays out over five years rather than upfront. If you want a check at install time, look at Illinois, Oregon or Connecticut instead.

Florida — no state rebate, but exemptions and hurricane math

Florida is the clearest case where a state with strong battery demand (hurricane outages, 1.2 million homes with solar) offers almost no state-level battery incentive. What you get is exemptions and a few local utility programs.

  • No state solar or battery rebate, no state tax credit: Florida does not run a statewide battery rebate program and has no state income tax credit for storage.
  • Sales tax + property tax exemptions: solar and storage equipment is exempt from sales tax, and the added value of a solar-plus-storage system is excluded from property tax assessment. On a $15,000 system, the sales tax exemption alone is roughly $1,000 saved.
  • Local utility programs: JEA (Jacksonville) has offered up to $4,000 for qualifying battery storage; the City of Tallahassee and a few other municipal utilities run smaller pilots. FPL and Duke Energy Florida do not offer a standard residential battery rebate.
  • The hurricane argument: Florida's case for a battery is resilience, not rebates. If each storm season costs you a fridge of food or a day of work, the backup value stands on its own — just don't expect the state to help pay for it.

Florida is included here because it is one of the largest home-battery markets in the country and homeowners search for incentives that, honestly, barely exist. Better to say that plainly than to let an installer's quote imply otherwise.

How to verify your own state and utility

  • Start with DSIRE (dsireusa.org) for the official list of state and utility programs in your area — it is the most comprehensive public database, though entries can lag program closures.
  • Then check your specific utility — especially if it is a municipal or cooperative utility, which often run their own rebates outside the state program (as in California's SMUD/Riverside/Pasadena and Texas's Austin Energy).
  • Ask for an itemized quote that lists each incentive separately with its program name, not a single "net after incentives" number. A rebate labeled "SGIP" on a 2026 California IOU quote, or "federal credit" on any 2026 purchase quote, is a red flag.
  • Confirm the program is open and funded today, not just listed. SGIP's general-market budget is the clearest example of a program that still appears on lists but is closed to new applicants.
  • Check retention and clawback rules — Illinois's 5-year rule and California's demand-response enrollment requirement both tie the rebate to ongoing participation.

What this means for your payback

State and utility incentives can move payback by years — or by nothing at all, depending on where you live. A $4,000 Illinois rebate or a $5,000 New York credit on a $15,000 battery shortens payback substantially; the same battery on a closed California state program with no municipal utility rebate gets $0 from this layer and depends entirely on your electricity rate structure.

Quick answers

Which state pays the most for a home battery in 2026?

For a standard-income homeowner buying outright, Illinois ($300/kWh, ~$4,050 on a 13.5 kWh battery) and California's municipal utilities ($500–550/kWh) offer the largest upfront rebates. New York offers up to $5,000 as a state tax credit. California's state SGIP program pays $0 for standard-income households in 2026 — the value moved to city-owned utilities.

Is the California SGIP battery rebate still available?

Not for standard-income households. As of October 7, 2026, SGIP's general-market Small Residential Storage budget ($150/kWh) and Equity Resiliency budget ($1,000/kWh) are closed statewide. Low-income households can join the Residential Solar and Storage Equity waitlist ($1.10/Wh for storage), and municipal utilities like SMUD, Riverside and Pasadena run their own active rebates.

Do any states still have a state tax credit for batteries?

Yes. New York offers 25% of system cost up to $5,000, Maryland 30% of installation cost up to $5,000 (first-come, first-served), and Hawaii's RETITC covers 35% of system cost up to $5,000 — including storage retrofits since 2026. Massachusetts and some other states use pay-for-performance programs (SMART 3.0, ConnectedSolutions) rather than an upfront tax credit. Most states have no state-level battery tax credit.

Can I stack a state rebate with a lease/PPA federal credit?

Not directly — the Section 48E credit for leased/PPA systems goes to the system owner (the company), not to you, and state rebates for third-party-owned systems are often structured differently. If you lease, ask the provider how each incentive is reflected in your monthly payment. This page's rebates assume you buy the system outright.
