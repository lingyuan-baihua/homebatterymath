Solar + Battery vs Solar Alone: What the Battery Actually Adds in 2026

Battery attachment is now around 40% nationally and 94% in California. Here’s how a battery changes solar payback in 2026 — export rates, self-consumption math, and when panels alone still win.

8 min read Updated November 2026 Sources linked at the bottom

Batteries are no longer an accessory to solar — nationally about 40% of new residential solar systems include one, and in California the attachment rate is around 94%. The reason is simple: in many markets, exporting surplus power now pays very little, while using it yourself is worth full retail.

Short answer A battery adds the most value where exports are cheap and retail rates are high (California under NEM 3.0). It adds less where net metering still pays retail for exports. In every market, it also buys outage protection — value that doesn’t show up in a payback calculation.

What changed in 2026

  • The 30% federal credit is gone for owner-purchased systems placed in service after December 31, 2025 — for panels and batteries alike. Quotes that still assume it are wrong. What battery tax credits still apply in 2026 →
  • Export rates have fallen. Under California’s NEM 3.0, exported solar earns roughly $0.05–$0.08/kWh, while on-site consumption offsets retail rates around $0.30–$0.55/kWh.
  • Battery costs have settled. A typical 13.5 kWh battery still runs about $13,000–$16,500 installed in the U.S. in 2026.

The math: export vs self-consumption

Take a home that sends 10 kWh of daytime solar to the evening. Without a battery, that energy is exported at the avoided-cost rate. With a battery, it offsets retail power instead:

PathValue per kWhAnnual value (10 kWh/day)
Export to grid (NEM 3.0)~$0.06~$219
Store and self-consume~$0.35~$1,278
Difference~$0.29~$1,059

At a $14,500 installed battery, that difference alone implies a ~14-year marginal payback — which is why battery decisions usually need either a wider rate spread, a local rebate, or backup value to work. System-level comparisons look better because the battery also raises the value of the solar you already own: in California, solar-plus-battery payback is commonly estimated at 6–9 years versus 10–14 years for solar alone. When a battery clearly helps

  • Cheap exports, expensive evenings. NEM 3.0-style billing makes self-consumption the whole game.
  • Time-of-use rates with a wide spread. The battery can also arbitrage grid power, not just solar.
  • Outages or public-safety shutoffs. Backup value is real, even if it never appears in the payback.
  • State or utility rebates. A meaningful local incentive can pull payback inside the warranty.

When panels alone may still win: full-retail net metering (many states still offer 1:1), flat low rates, or a tight budget where the battery’s marginal return is negative.

Quick answers

Does a battery make solar cheaper?

It makes the energy you already generate more valuable when exports are cheap. On a 1:1 net-metering plan, the financial gain is smaller — the case shifts toward backup and time-of-use arbitrage.

How big a battery do I need for my solar?

Size for evening consumption, not array size. A common rule is to cover 30–50% of daily use; a 12 kWh evening load suggests a 13.5 kWh battery. Use the size calculator to work from your own numbers.
