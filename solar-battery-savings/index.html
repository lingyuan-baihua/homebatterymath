How this calculator works (methodology & assumptions)

+ capacity × remaining cycles × (peak − off-peak) × efficiency

  • System-size mode estimates surplus as: production ≈ kWp × 4.5 h/day, of which ≈55% is exported without storage (typical split; your meter reading is more accurate).
  • Surplus valuation: stored energy is valued at the peak rate under TOU (it offsets your most expensive evening energy first), or the flat rate otherwise.
  • Remaining cycles after solar charging can still arbitrage the grid under TOU rates.
  • Excluded: export tariffs, demand charges, financing, tax on savings.

Is a battery worth adding to your solar?

honest payback math for 2026 — with the federal credit gone

With the federal 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) expired for owner-purchased systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, the battery payback question got harder. A battery still makes sense for many solar owners — but only where rate structure rewards self-consumption. Here is where the math lands in 2026.

Solar-only vs solar + battery payback (2026)

SetupTypical paybackWhy
Solar only (California, cash)4–5 yearsPanels cheap; export credits alone still recover cost fast
Solar + battery (California, cash)8–9 yearsBattery adds ~4 years; self-consumption offsets high peak rates
Solar only under NEM 3.09–13 yearsExport earns ~$0.05–0.08/kWh — barely worth sending back
Solar + battery under NEM 3.06–9 yearsBattery captures peak-rate energy instead of exporting at a pittance

Ranges are 2026 cash-purchase estimates for typical systems; your numbers depend on your rate, surplus and installed price — run them in the calculator above. The federal credit ending added roughly 2–3 years to payback versus 2025.

When a battery clearly pays off

  • Wide time-of-use spread. If your peak rate is 3–5× your off-peak rate, a battery that shifts evening load pays back fastest. California, Hawaii and parts of the Northeast fit this profile.
  • NEM 3.0 territory (California). Exports earn so little that storing your own surplus for evening use is almost always better than sending it to the grid.
  • Frequent outages with real cost. If each outage costs you a fridge of food or a workday, backup value stacks on top of bill savings.
  • VPP enrollment available. Virtual power plant payments ($400–$1,500/year in some California programs) shorten payback — but treat as upside, not the reason to buy.

When a battery probably isn't worth it

  • Flat, low electricity rate. If you pay one cheap rate all day, there's no spread to arbitrage — bill savings alone rarely recover the battery cost.
  • Generous net metering (NEM 1/2 or legacy). If exports earn near retail, a battery adds little; solar-only wins on payback.
  • Little evening consumption. A battery only helps if you actually use the stored energy after sundown.

For the policy detail behind these numbers, read the NEM 3.0 battery savings guide or the solar + battery vs solar alone guide.
